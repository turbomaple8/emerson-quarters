Coliving in Seattle Through the Winter: What November to March Is Actually Like

2026-10-20 · Part of Coliving in Seattle

People choose a co-living building in summer and live in it in winter. In Seattle those are different experiences, and the features that matter are not the ones that sell a room in July.

Here is what November through March actually does to shared living here, and what to weigh differently if you are choosing a room now.

What a Seattle winter is, precisely

Not cold in a dramatic sense. Grey, damp, and continuous.

The rain is light and more or less constant from November into March. It rarely stops you doing anything; it is simply always slightly happening. And it gets dark early — by late afternoon in December, you are walking home in the dark from an afternoon class or a 5pm finish.

Two consequences that reshape a building's value: everything you own is slightly wet, and everyone is indoors more.

What stops mattering

The balcony. From June to September a private balcony is the best thing in the unit. From November it is storage. Worth having — it comes back in spring and it is still somewhere that is only yours — but do not let it decide your choice if you are moving in October.

The ten-minute walk you accepted in September. This is the big one. A pleasant walk at the end of summer is the thing you resent by February, three times a day, in the dark, in drizzle. Distance is the feature that quietly changes value most between seasons.

Proximity to nightlife. If you chose Capitol Hill for the scene, winter is when you find out how much of the scene you actually use on a wet Tuesday. Some people: a lot. Others discover they paid for optionality they never exercise.

What starts mattering

On-site laundry. In summer this is a convenience. From November it is the difference between drying your things and carrying wet clothes out of the building — and in Queen Anne, which is steep enough that the city once ran a counterbalance up the south face for streetcars, "out of the building" can mean up a hill.

Somewhere for wet outdoor things. Coats, shoes, umbrellas. Every shared unit needs an agreed spot, or the hallway becomes it by default and nobody enjoys that. Worth settling in October rather than discovering in January.

A desk and a lamp in your own room. Overhead lighting at four in the afternoon in a Seattle December is genuinely demoralising. A private room with a desk, a chair and a lamp is where winter is survivable. At Emerson Quarters the desk and chair come with the room, so this is not a thing you postpone buying until it is too dark to care.

A shared kitchen people actually use. In summer everyone eats out and the kitchen is incidental. In winter it is where the building's social life happens, because nobody is going anywhere. A full kitchen — stove, refrigerator, microwave, counters — stops being a line on an amenity list.

Which side of the water you are on. Seattle Pacific sits directly on the ship canal. Anything north of it crosses the Fremont Bridge, which opens for marine traffic on its own schedule. In July, waiting for a boat is a minor annoyance. In February, in the rain, in the dark, with a bag, it is the thing that makes you move.

The social half

Shared living gets harder and better in winter at the same time.

Harder: everyone is inside, the common spaces are busier, and there is nowhere to go to be alone except your own room. That is precisely why the room needs to be a room you want to be in, with a door that shuts.

Better: a building where people are home is a building where you know your housemates. Summer units are empty; winter units have someone in the kitchen. If you arrived in autumn and have not met the person two doors down, you will by January.

What turnover looks like here specifically

Near Seattle Pacific, the building's rhythm follows the university's. SPU runs quarters — Autumn from late September, Winter from early January, Spring from late March — so turnover happens at quarter boundaries rather than once each September.

Practically, that means the first week of January is a change-of-cast moment in a way it is not at a semester school. Some people finish in December. New ones arrive for Winter quarter. If the mix in your unit was not working, it changes. If it was, you lose some of it.

Worth knowing before you read January as a verdict on the building.

If you are choosing a room in October

Weight these differently than the summer version of yourself would:

  1. Distance to where you go daily — over-weight this, heavily
  2. Which side of the ship canal, and where on the hill — the two obstacles that do not appear on a map
  3. On-site laundry — now a real feature
  4. A private room with a desk, chair and lamp — this is where your winter happens
  5. Balcony and view — enjoy them in May; do not let them decide October
