What a Seattle Internship Actually Pays After Housing

2026-08-17 · Part of Summer Intern Housing in Seattle

Interns compare offers on the headline number, which is the one figure guaranteed not to tell them what they will actually have. Two things move it far more: tax, and what housing costs where you can realistically live.

Seattle is unusual on the first and misunderstood on the second.

Washington has no state income tax

This is the single largest thing people miss when comparing a Seattle offer against New York or California.

In New York you would pay state and city income tax on top of federal. In California, state income tax. In Washington, neither — federal only.

On an intern salary that is not a rounding error. Two offers with the same headline number, one in Seattle and one in New York, do not produce the same amount of money in your account, and the gap is large enough to swing a decision.

Run your comparison on take-home, not gross. Seattle rents look high next to some cities until you do that, and then the picture changes.

The second number: what you can realistically live on

Here the reasoning that works for a year-long lease breaks down, because an internship is ten to fourteen weeks.

The options, honestly:

Company-arranged housing. Amazon and Microsoft both route interns through corporate housing providers — ABODA is the long-standing one in this market. If it is in your offer, it is the least effort: furnished, sorted, done. What you give up is choice about where and what, and it is usually priced against the stipend rather than the open market.

University summer housing. UW's housing office runs a summer intern programme with furnished rooms and a minimum stay of around 28 nights; Cornish Commons also sells summer beds. Built for exactly your situation, terms that fit. They fill early, and they put you in student accommodation for a summer when you are not a student.

A sublet. Cheapest and least certain. You are trusting photographs and a stranger's account, usually with money sent before you land.

A room in a co-living building. More than a sublet, less than corporate housing, and the building still exists and is still run by someone when something goes wrong at 11pm.

The cost that does not appear on any listing

Commute time, and on a twelve-week stay it is the expensive one.

Forty-five minutes each way over twelve weeks is roughly ninety hours — more than two full working weeks of your time in Seattle, spent travelling. Against that, the monthly difference between a cheap room far out and a closer one is usually small.

And Seattle makes commutes less predictable than most cities, because it is cut up by water and hills:

  • South Lake Union — where much of the city's tech employment sits — is immediately south-east of Queen Anne. Short, and predictable, with no bridge in the way.
  • Downtown is around ten minutes by car.
  • Redmond means the 520 bridge across Lake Washington, twice a day. The saving grace is direction: the heavy morning flow is westbound into Seattle, so you would be reverse-commuting and moving against the queue. It is still a bridge, and bridges have no alternative route.

Putting it together

The comparison worth running, for each offer:

  1. Take-home, not gross — this is where Washington's no-income-tax position shows up
  2. Minus rent for somewhere you can actually reach without a long commute
  3. Minus setup — furniture, deposits, utility accounts. A furnished all-in room removes this entirely; an empty room does not, and you will not recover the cost in twelve weeks
  4. Minus the commute, valued honestly in hours

Offers that look similar on line one often separate sharply by line four.
