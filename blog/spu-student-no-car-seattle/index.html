Getting Around Seattle Without a Car as an SPU Student

2026-10-30 · Part of Student Housing Near Seattle Pacific University

Most SPU students who bring a car to Seattle spend the first quarter discovering they did not need it, and the rest of the year paying for it anyway.

This is the arithmetic, and the honest list of the times you will miss one.

What a car costs here, beyond the car

Insurance, fuel and maintenance are the obvious part. The Seattle-specific part is parking, and it is the one that decides it.

Street parking on Queen Anne is a hill-and-permit problem rather than a simple availability problem. On-site parking, where a building has it, is the clean answer — Emerson Quarters has it, first-come first-served — but you are paying for a thing that spends most of the week stationary.

Then there is the part nobody budgets: the time you spend moving the car rather than being somewhere. In a neighbourhood where your campus is a three-minute walk, a car can quietly become a chore with wheels.

What you do not need a car for

Campus. Three minutes on foot from 608 W Emerson St. This is the whole reason the rest of the list is manageable — the trip you make most often is the one that needs nothing.

Groceries and everyday things. Queen Anne Avenue's shops and restaurants are about eight minutes on foot.

Downtown and Seattle Center. Metro stops are roughly two minutes from the building. Seattle Center is walkable on a decent day; downtown is a short ride.

South Lake Union. If you work or intern there alongside studying, it is close enough that most people making that trip do not keep a car.

Fremont. Across the ship canal. Walkable over the bridge when you are going deliberately — just not something you want in your daily commute, because the Fremont Bridge is a drawbridge and opens for marine traffic on its own schedule.

The quarter rhythm makes this easier than it sounds

SPU runs quarters, not semesters — Autumn from late September, Winter from early January, Spring from late March, each about ten weeks.

Two consequences for transport:

Your schedule resets three times a year. A timetable you hate is ten weeks long, not fifteen. If Autumn gave you an awkward gap that made you wish for a car, Winter probably will not.

The expensive trips cluster. Move-in, move-out, and the trip home at a quarter break. Those are the moments a car is genuinely useful — and they are three or four days a year, not a daily need. Renting or a ride for those days costs a fraction of owning.

When you will actually miss one

Honestly, because "you don't need a car in Seattle" is usually oversold:

Moving in and out. The one real case. A furnished room removes most of it — if the bed, desk, chair, storage and TV are already there, you are moving suitcases rather than furniture — but there is still a trip.

Getting out of the city. The mountains, the coast, Mount Rainier. This is the genuine loss. Seattle's best weekends are often an hour's drive away, and transit does not serve them well. Most people solve it by knowing someone with a car or renting for the day.

Very late nights. Metro thins out. Not a crisis, but plan it rather than discover it.

Grocery runs in February. You can carry groceries eight minutes in the rain. You will not enjoy it every time.

What makes the no-car version work

Three things, in order of how much they matter:

  1. Live on the right side of the water. If campus is across the ship canal from you, every class involves a drawbridge. This is not a preference, it is structural.
  2. Live off the steep face of the hill. Queen Anne is steep enough that the city once built a counterbalance to get streetcars up it. Which side of the crest you are on changes a walk more than the distance does.
  3. Be close enough that you do not deliberate. At twenty minutes, every trip is a decision. At three, it is not.

Everything else — transit card, a decent raincoat, knowing the two bus routes you actually use — is detail.
