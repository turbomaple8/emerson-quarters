Arranging housing from another country adds two problems to an already awkward one. You are committing to a room you cannot visit, and you are committing on a timeline that is partly out of your hands because of a visa.

Here is how the sequence actually works, and what it costs.

## The timing problem, and how to not be trapped by it

The tension: good rooms go early, and you may not have certainty about your dates until late.

The mistake is treating those as the same deadline. They are not.

**Offers for a Seattle summer land between September and December.** Housing searches peak January to April. Visa processing sits somewhere in between and is the part you do not control.

## What the options cost, honestly

The four routes, from an international intern's position:

**Company-arranged housing.** If your offer includes it, take it seriously — it removes the entire problem and it is the only option where someone else carries the risk of your dates moving. Amazon and Microsoft both route interns through corporate providers. The cost is choice: you take what you are assigned.

**University summer housing.** UW's housing office and Cornish Commons both sell summer beds, with minimum stays around 28 nights. Built for your situation. They fill early, which is the catch when your dates are uncertain.

**A sublet.** Cheapest headline, worst fit for your position. You are sending money across borders to a private individual for a room you cannot see, with no recourse if it is not as described. From abroad, this is where the risk concentrates.

**A room in a co-living building.** Rooms at Emerson Quarters start at **$895 a month**, all-in. For comparison, apartment rents in Queen Anne commonly run **$3,400 to $5,200**, which is why a room is how most people are in this neighbourhood at all. The relevant difference from a sublet is not the price — it is that the building is operated by someone who is still there when your flight is delayed or your dates shift.

## The cost line nobody abroad accounts for

Setup.

If you arrive to an empty room, you need a bed, a mattress, a desk, a chair, storage and bedding before you can sleep properly. That is several hundred dollars and a weekend — in a country where you have no local credit history, possibly no bank account yet, and a twelve-week runway.

Then you sell it all for a fraction, in your final week, while also leaving.

A furnished room removes that line entirely. ## Paying safely from abroad

This matters more for you than for a domestic intern, because recourse is harder across a border.

- **Do not send money before a live conversation.** Not a recorded video, a live call where you can ask to see specific things.
- **Verify the operator exists outside the listing** — an address, a phone number a human answers, a building with a name.
- **Be wary of any method with no recourse** — wire to a personal account, gift cards, crypto, cash apps.
- **Get the hold terms in writing**, including what happens if your visa decision moves your dates.
- **Ask what happens if you cannot come at all.** The answer tells you a lot about who you are dealing with.

A hold that costs nothing is the right shape. Money moving before a conversation is the wrong one.

## Questions worth asking on that call

- Can the dates flex to an internship rather than an academic year? Standard terms here are six or twelve months, with shorter sometimes available — ask about yours specifically.
- What is the street address, and what is between it and my office?
- How many rooms share the kitchen and the bathroom?
- Is the rate a floor or a flat rate?
- What is the deposit, and what do you need from someone without US credit history?

The last two are the ones this page cannot answer for you — they depend on the room and on your situation. Ask them directly.
