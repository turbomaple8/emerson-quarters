Co-living is marketed at students, which makes most professionals skip past it. That is a reasonable reaction to the marketing and a poor reaction to the product.

If you are earning a Seattle salary, the argument for renting a room is different from the student one — and so are the reasons it might not suit you.

## The student argument does not apply to you

The usual case is affordability: a room is what you can reach when an apartment is not.

That is not your situation. You could rent a one-bedroom. So the question is not "can I afford more" but **"what would more actually buy me, and do I want it?"**

Three honest answers people arrive at:

**You are new to Seattle and do not know where you want to live yet.** Signing a twelve-month lease on a neighbourhood you have seen for a weekend is a real commitment. A room lets you live here for six months and then decide with information.

**You are here on a defined stint.** A contract, a rotation, a project. The overhead of furnishing and unfurnishing an apartment for fourteen months is disproportionate.

**You would rather have the money than the square footage.** Apartment rents in Queen Anne commonly run **$3,400 to $5,200 a month** against **$895** for a room. That delta is not a small lifestyle adjustment; over a year it is a different financial position. Some people want the apartment. Some would rather have the difference.

## What the product actually is at this price point

Worth stating plainly, because "co-living" covers a wide range.

A private, lockable bedroom — furnished with a bed, desk, chair, nightstand, storage and a wall-mounted TV. A full kitchen shared with the others in your unit: stove, refrigerator, microwave, granite counters. Shared bathroom. On-site laundry. On-site parking. Many units have a private balcony.

One rate covers water, electricity, heat, garbage and high-speed WiFi. You sign for your room, not the unit, so you are responsible for your rent and not anyone else's.

That is the whole thing. It is not a hotel and it is not a studio.

## Where it goes wrong for professionals specifically

This is the part the marketing does not cover, and it is what you actually need.

**Calls from your room.** You will take them there, because there is nowhere else. The room needs to be somewhere you are willing to be seen on video and able to think in. A desk and a door that shuts is the minimum, and it is why a furnished room with an actual desk matters more than it sounds.

**Different hours from the building.** If your day starts at seven and the building's centre of gravity is students on a later timetable, you will notice. Worth asking who is in the unit before you sign — not as a judgement, as a scheduling question.

**Hosting.** You can have people over; you cannot host the way you would in your own place. If that matters to you, it matters.

**Guests staying.** Ask the policy specifically. It varies by operator and it is the thing people forget to check until they need it.

## What makes it work for professionals

**The commute, if the geography is right.** This is the one that converts people. Seattle is cut up by water and hills, so being on the correct side of an obstacle is worth more than an extra room. Queen Anne sits immediately north-west of South Lake Union and around ten minutes from downtown — close enough that most residents working there do not keep a car.

**The predictability.** One number, once a month, including the utilities that spike from November through March when the heat runs continuously. No accounts to open, no bills to split, no month where the total surprises you.

**The exit.** Six or twelve month terms, and you leave with a suitcase. No furniture to sell on the week you are moving.

**Not being alone in a new city.** Underrated. Moving to Seattle for a job and signing a studio lease is an efficient way to spend your first six months not meeting anyone. A shared unit is a low-effort default social floor.

## The honest test

Ask yourself what you would do with the extra space.

If you have a specific answer — I cook elaborately, I have a home gym, my partner is moving too, I need a second room for work — take the apartment. Queen Anne will charge you for it, and it will be worth it.

If the answer is vague, you are paying several thousand dollars a year for the *idea* of space. That is a legitimate thing to buy. It is just worth knowing that is what you are buying.
