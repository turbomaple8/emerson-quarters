Shared living rarely goes wrong dramatically. It goes wrong slowly, through small things nobody addressed in week one because addressing them felt awkward — and by month three the awkwardness has compounded into an atmosphere.

The good news is that the fix is early and small. Here is what is worth sorting in the first week, and what genuinely is not.

## Sort this in week one

**A shelf that is yours.** In the fridge and in a cupboard. Not because anyone is going to steal your food, but because "whose is this" is the single most reliable source of low-grade friction in a shared kitchen, and it disappears entirely once everything has an owner.

**When people actually leave in the morning.** Not a rota — just knowing. At Emerson Quarters the mix helps: campus is three minutes away, so SPU students leave on a class timetable, while people commuting downtown or to South Lake Union are on office hours. Departures stagger themselves. Ten minutes of conversation tells you where you fit and removes the bathroom question for the rest of the year.

**Your own washing-up habit.** The thing that breaks a shared kitchen is never the schedule; it is a pan left "for later". Decide in week one that you wash yours before leaving the room, and the question never becomes a conversation.

**Say when you are around.** People who work nights, study late, or keep unusual hours do fine in shared units — as long as everyone knows. Unannounced difference reads as inconsiderate; announced difference is just information.

## Do not bother with this

**A cleaning rota.** They almost never survive contact with a real month, and proposing one in week one reads as an accusation. What works is doing your own share visibly and early. It sets a norm faster than a spreadsheet enforces one.

**Becoming friends immediately.** You might, and that is a bonus. But shared living works fine between people who are simply considerate, and forcing closeness in week one is more likely to produce polite avoidance later. Aim for easy, not close.

**Optimising your room on day one.** You do not know yet where the light is good in the afternoon, or which corner is quietest. Live in it a week first. At Emerson the room comes furnished — bed, desk, chair, nightstand, storage, wall-mounted TV — so there is nothing to buy while you work that out.

## The Seattle-specific parts

**Sort the drying question before November.** From roughly November through March it rains more or less continuously here. Wet coats, wet shoes, wet everything, all needing somewhere to go. On-site laundry helps — wet clothes never leave the building, or go up the Queen Anne hill — but the thing to agree early is where wet outdoor things live, because the hallway becomes the default and nobody enjoys that.

**Expect the light to change your evenings.** Seattle winters get dark early. A unit that feels sociable in September can feel like five closed doors in January, simply because everyone is inside by four. It is worth knowing that in advance and not reading it as anything personal. Many units here have private balconies, which help more than you would expect.

**Learn the quarter rhythm.** SPU runs quarters rather than semesters — Autumn from late September, Winter from early January, Spring from late March — so the building turns over at quarter boundaries rather than once a year in September. Practically: your housemates may change more often than at a semester school, and January is a normal time for someone new to arrive. The upside is that being the new person is unremarkable here. Someone else was new eight weeks ago.

## What is yours regardless

Your bedroom, and it locks.

That is the thing that makes the rest work. Shared living goes wrong when there is nowhere in the building that is only yours — and here there is. The kitchen and bathroom are shared facilities attached to a private room, not the other way round. If a week is going badly, you close a door.
