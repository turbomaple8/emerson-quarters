If you are moving to Seattle for a summer internship or a first job, the decision that shapes your months here is not the room. It is where the room is relative to the office — and Seattle punishes that choice more than most cities, because it is cut up by water and hills rather than laid out on a grid.

Here is what the commute from Queen Anne actually looks like to the three places people usually work.

## South Lake Union: the short one

South Lake Union is where a large share of Seattle's tech employment sits, Amazon's campus most visibly. It is immediately south-east of Queen Anne — close enough that from 608 W Emerson St the trip is short and, more importantly, **predictable**.

Predictable is the operative word. There is no bridge in the way and no water to go around. You are not gambling on a drawbridge or a lake crossing; you are travelling a couple of miles inside the same side of the city.

Most people making this commute do not keep a car. Between Metro stops about two minutes from the building and the distance involved, parking in SLU costs more than it solves.

## Downtown: about ten minutes by car

Downtown Seattle and Seattle Center are both roughly ten minutes by car, and well served by bus. Seattle Center in particular — the Space Needle, the theatres, the campus of museums — is close enough to be a walk on a good day.

For anyone working downtown, Queen Anne is one of the few residential neighbourhoods that is genuinely close without being *in* the commercial core, which is the trade most people are looking for and rarely find at a room price.

## Redmond: the one to think about

Microsoft and much of the Eastside are in Redmond, which is across Lake Washington. That means the **520 bridge**, every day, twice.

There is one genuine saving grace, and it is worth understanding because it is counterintuitive: **you would be reverse-commuting.** The heavy morning flow is westbound, *into* Seattle. Going eastbound in the morning and westbound in the evening puts you against the direction of the queue rather than in it. You will spend a fair amount of your commute looking across the barrier at traffic that is not moving.

It is still a bridge crossing twice a day, and bridges over water have no alternative route when something goes wrong. If your office is in Redmond, be honest with yourself about whether you want a lake between you and your bed for twelve weeks.

## The two obstacles nobody mentions in listings

**The ship canal.** Seattle Pacific University sits directly on it, and the Fremont Bridge crosses it. That bridge is a drawbridge over a working waterway — it opens for marine traffic, not for your schedule. A room in Fremont that looks a ten-minute walk from campus on a map can cost you twenty-five minutes on a morning when a sailboat is coming through. If your route crosses it daily, you will eventually be late because of a boat.

**The hill.** Queen Anne is steep. Steep enough that the city once ran a counterbalance system up the south face to help streetcars climb it. Distance on a Queen Anne map is not the same as distance on foot, and which side of the crest you live on genuinely changes your day — particularly from November onward, when it rains more or less continuously.

608 W Emerson St sits on the campus side of the canal and off the steep face of the hill. That is a smaller thing to say than it is to live with.

## The arithmetic worth doing

The mistake people make on a short stay is optimising for rent and inheriting a commute.

Over a twelve-week internship, forty-five minutes each way is roughly **ninety hours** — more than two full working weeks of your time in Seattle, spent in transit. On a stay that short, paying somewhat more to be close is almost always the better trade, and the maths does not need to be close for it to hold.

## One piece of Seattle arithmetic in your favour

If you are comparing a Seattle offer against one in New York or California, do not compare the headline salary. **Washington has no state income tax.**

In New York you would be paying state *and* city income tax on top of federal. Here you pay neither. On an intern salary that difference is real money, and it lands in your account rather than in a calculation you never see.

Run your numbers on take-home against rent, not gross against rent. Seattle rents look high until you do.
