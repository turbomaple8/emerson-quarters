"Furnished" removes the big purchases. It does not remove the small ones, and the small ones are what make the first week comfortable or irritating.

If you are moving into a room in Queen Anne between November and March, this is what is already there, what is not, and what this particular season asks for.

## What is not in the room

The predictable gaps:

**Bedding.** Sheets, duvet, pillows. A furnished room gives you a bed, not a made one. This is the single most common first-night problem.

**Towels.**

**Kitchen things that are yours.** Pots and pans may or may not be shared; your own mug, plate and cutlery are worth having regardless, if only so nothing is ambiguous.

**A lamp.** Overhead lighting is not enough for working, and in a Seattle December it is dark by mid-afternoon. More on this below.

**Toiletries, cleaning things, the usual.**

## What a Seattle winter specifically asks for

This is the part a generic moving list will not tell you, and it is the difference between a comfortable first month and a damp one.

**A drying rack.** It rains lightly and more or less continuously from November through March. Your coat, shoes and bag will be damp most days. On-site laundry means wet clothes never leave the building — but between wearing and washing, things need somewhere to dry that is not the back of a chair.

**Waterproof shoes you can walk in.** Note both halves. Queen Anne is steep enough that the city once ran a counterbalance up the south face to get streetcars over it, and you will be walking it. Shoes that are waterproof but uncomfortable get left by the door.

**A proper rain jacket, not an umbrella.** Locals mostly do not use umbrellas. The rain is light and persistent rather than heavy, and a hood handles it better on a hill in wind.

**A desk lamp.** Repeating it because it matters more than it sounds. By December it is dark by mid-afternoon, and a room lit only from the ceiling at four in the afternoon is genuinely grim to work in. This is the cheapest purchase on the list and the one people most regret skipping.

**A door mat or a spot for wet shoes.** Agree where wet outdoor things live with your housemates in the first week, before the hallway becomes the default.

## What not to bring

**Furniture.** Obviously, but people still try. The room is furnished and your room is one room.

**A car, probably.** Campus is three minutes' walk, Metro stops about two, Queen Anne Avenue eight on foot, downtown roughly ten minutes by car. On-site parking exists if you want it, but between the hill, the weather and the parking situation, most people here are better without one.

**Three months of anything.** You are moving into a neighbourhood with shops eight minutes away. Buy the bulky things after you arrive, not in a suitcase.

## The arrival-week order

What to do in roughly this sequence:

1. **Bedding, first day.** Everything else can wait a night; this cannot.
2. **Lamp and drying rack.** The two purchases that change the room most for the least money.
3. **Walk your daily route once, in the actual weather.** Find out what it is really like before you need to do it on a schedule.
4. **Claim a shelf** in the fridge and a cupboard. Ten minutes of clarity that prevents months of low-grade friction.
5. **Say hello properly**, and mention your rough hours. Easier in week one than in week six.

## Why furnished matters more in winter than in summer

A September move-in with an empty room is a project you can do in pleasant weather over a weekend.

A January move-in with an empty room means carrying flat-pack furniture up a Queen Anne hill in the rain, assembling it in a cold room, and sleeping on a floor in the meantime. The same task, several times harder, in the month when you least want it.

If you are moving between November and March, the furnished option is not a convenience. It is most of the reason the move is manageable at all.
